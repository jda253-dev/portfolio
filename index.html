Case Study 01 · Behavioral Research

Yale Brain & Cognition Lab — Internal Attention Study

Designed and refined an object-naming experiment to study how semantic and phonetic relationships influence attention and processing.

Experimental DesignPsychoPy / PsychoJSParticipant ResearchBehavioral AnalysisIteration
Role
Research Intern
Setting
Yale University
Focus
Attention & Language
Tools
PsychoPy, JS, R

The question

How do different kinds of information shape what people attend to and how quickly they can retrieve a response? The project required converting an abstract cognitive question into a task that participants could understand and complete reliably.

What I did

I helped develop the experiment from stimulus selection through implementation and testing. I built and refined task logic in PsychoPy/PsychoJS, worked on timing, randomization, counterbalancing, microphone capture, stimulus matching, and online deployment through Pavlovia. I also supported participant data collection and behavioral analysis.

Human-centered decisions

A major part of the work was noticing when the task itself could create noise: unclear instructions, timing that felt unnatural, inconsistent stimulus properties, or technical friction in the online experience. I tested these issues, adjusted the study, and re-checked the participant flow before data collection.

What I learned

Good research is not just about choosing the right statistical test. The quality of the evidence depends on whether the experience makes sense to the participant. This project strengthened my ability to separate a research question from the mechanics of the interface, then iterate until the task measures what it is supposed to measure.

UX takeaway: ambiguous behavioral questions become actionable only after the research experience is designed carefully enough to produce trustworthy evidence.

Case Study 02 · Multimodal Research

Cornell AMP Lab — Attention, Memory & Event Segmentation

Supported eye-tracking, EEG, and behavioral studies examining how people perceive and segment ongoing events.

Role

Setting
Cornell University
Focus

Methods

The problem

Understanding attention in the real world requires more than asking people what they noticed. The lab combines behavioral data with eye tracking and EEG to study how people process continuous experiences.

What I did

I helped run participant sessions, followed precise experimental protocols, supported eye-tracking and EEG setup, organized research data, and worked with computational video features used in event-segmentation research.

Why this matters for UX

The work taught me to think beyond self-report. Where someone looks, when attention shifts, and how behavior changes over time can reveal things a participant may never articulate directly. That perspective is useful when studying complex interfaces, workflows, and moments of friction.

What I learned

Reliable user evidence depends on disciplined data collection. Small inconsistencies in setup or instructions can undermine a study, so I learned to balance participant comfort with protocol fidelity and data quality.

UX takeaway: observed behavior can reveal attention patterns and friction that interviews alone may miss.

Research lens

1
PrepareSet up equipment and protocols consistently across sessions.
2
ObserveCapture behavioral, gaze, and physiological signals.
3
ValidateCheck data quality and identify possible procedural issues.
4
InterpretConnect multimodal evidence to questions about attention and event structure.
Case Study 03 · Product & Engineering

Quadyster — Internal Application Development

Built an internal application while working across front-end, back-end, debugging, and user-facing product decisions.

The challenge

Build an internal tool that worked reliably while keeping the user-facing flow understandable and efficient. The project required balancing technical constraints with the way people would actually move through the interface.

What I did

I developed and shipped application features using HTML, CSS, JavaScript, Bootstrap, Python Flask, and React Native. I collaborated with engineers to debug functionality, improve implementation, and make decisions about how technical behavior should appear to users.

How I approached it

Rather than treating the interface as a layer added after engineering, I thought about implementation and usability together: what information should appear first, where errors could occur, and how the product should respond when something went wrong.

What I learned

Product decisions are often constrained by what is technically feasible. Working directly with code gave me a better understanding of how design, engineering, and iteration influence one another—and made me more effective when collaborating across disciplines.

Product takeaway: the best user-facing solution is one that is both understandable to people and realistic to implement.
