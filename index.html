Yale Brain & Cognition Lab — Internal Attention Study

Research-heavy

The question

How do different kinds of information shape what people attend to and how quickly they can retrieve a response? The research problem was abstract; the participant experience had to make that problem measurable.

My contribution

I helped develop the study from stimulus selection through implementation and testing. I built and refined task logic in PsychoPy/PsychoJS, worked on timing, randomization, counterbalancing, microphone capture, stimulus matching, and online deployment through Pavlovia, then supported participant data collection and behavioral analysis.

The product-design problem inside the research problem

The interface could easily contaminate the evidence. Unclear instructions, unnatural timing, inconsistent stimuli, or technical friction could change participant behavior for the wrong reason. I treated those moments as usability problems: test the flow, isolate friction, change the experience, and re-check it before collecting data.

What changed in my thinking

I learned that a good research protocol behaves like a good product: the user should understand what to do, the interaction should feel coherent, and the system should produce interpretable feedback. Research quality and experience quality are often the same design problem viewed from different sides.

Key design decisions

1
Make the task legibleSeparate the scientific question from the mechanics participants actually see.
2
Control avoidable variationMatch stimuli and timing so the interface does not create confounds.
3
Prototype before collectingUse repeated testing to catch friction before it becomes noisy data.
OutcomeA more reliable participant flow and a stronger understanding of how interaction design affects the validity of behavioral evidence.

Cornell AMP Lab — Attention, Memory & Event Segmentation

Observation-heavy

I supported studies that combined behavior, gaze, physiology, and computational video features—training me to look for friction in what people do, not only in what they say.

The problem

Attention is difficult to understand through self-report alone. The lab combined behavioral data with eye tracking, EEG, and computational video features to study how people perceive and segment continuous experiences.

My contribution

I helped run participant sessions, followed precise experimental protocols, supported eye-tracking and EEG setup, organized research data, and worked with computational video features used in event-segmentation research.

What this taught me about users

People cannot always explain why something captured their attention or where a workflow became effortful. Observed behavior can reveal patterns that interviews miss. Gaze, timing, and shifts in behavior give another layer of evidence for understanding complex experiences.

Research discipline

Small inconsistencies in setup or instructions can undermine a study. I learned to balance participant comfort with protocol fidelity and to treat data quality as something designed into the research process—not checked only at the end.

How I would transfer this to product work

  • Use interviews for meaning, but behavior for validation.
  • Instrument key steps of a flow instead of relying on recall.
  • Look for attention shifts and drop-offs as evidence of friction.
  • Design the study so each signal answers a specific product question.
OutcomeA stronger research lens for combining qualitative interpretation with behavioral and physiological evidence.

The challenge

Build an internal tool that worked reliably while keeping the user-facing flow understandable and efficient. The work required balancing technical constraints with the way people would actually move through the interface.

My contribution

I developed and shipped application features using HTML, CSS, JavaScript, Bootstrap, Python Flask, and React Native. I collaborated with engineers to debug functionality, improve implementation, and make decisions about how technical behavior should appear to users.

How I approached the product

I did not treat the interface as a layer added after engineering. I considered implementation and usability together: what information should appear first, where errors could occur, how the system should communicate failure, and what the next action should be.

What I learned

Product decisions live inside technical constraints. Working directly with code made me better at evaluating which design ideas are realistic, communicating with engineers, and iterating on both functionality and user-facing behavior at the same time.

How I work

Research is useful when it changes a decision.

My process is intentionally simple: clarify what we need to learn, choose the lightest method that can answer it, and translate evidence into the next product move.

01

Frame the behavior

Turn a broad question into something observable: what is the user trying to do, where might friction occur, and what evidence would change our mind?

02

Prototype the learning

Build the smallest credible experience or study that exposes the behavior—whether that is a task flow, participant protocol, or working interface.

03

Make the decision clearer

Separate signal from noise, synthesize what matters, and explain what should change next and why.
