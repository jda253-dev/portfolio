Yale Brain & Cognition Lab / research + interaction

Designing a research experience that couldn’t afford ambiguity.

In a behavioral experiment, the interface is part of the measurement. I worked on the participant experience and browser implementation so instructions, timing, audio capture, and counterbalancing supported the science instead of adding noise.

01 / the real design problem

Scientific precision is a UX constraint.

Participants had to learn a subtle sequence, wait to respond until the correct moment, and complete the task in a browser with audio recording. Confusion or technical failure would not just be inconvenient — it could contaminate the behavior being measured.

CONSTRAINT 01

Teach without over-teaching.

Instructions needed to explain the cue clearly without nudging participants into a different strategy.

CONSTRAINT 02

Keep timing exact.

Fixation, encoding, cue, delay, and response phases had to feel simple while remaining experimentally controlled.

CONSTRAINT 03

Design for failure.

Microphone permissions, recording, uploads, and browser edge cases needed recovery paths before the main task.

02 / participant experience

Make the procedure visible before asking for performance.

Two artifacts from the study show the core interaction challenge: explain the full sequence at a glance, then make the cue behavior unmistakable enough to practice.

04 / key decisions

Three places where product thinking mattered.

The most important design work was not decorative. It was deciding where the interface should absorb complexity so the participant did not have to.

Decision 01

Separate learning from measurement.

I treated onboarding, learning, practice, and the main task as distinct states rather than one continuous instruction dump.

Why: lower procedural uncertainty before the data-collection phase begins.
Decision 02

Preflight the fragile part.

Microphone setup and response recording were handled before the core task, with room to retry rather than discovering failure after a session.

Why: technical reliability protects both participant confidence and usable data.
Decision 03

Make balance auditable.

The implementation logged the dimensions needed to inspect cue type, target position, condition, and expected responses rather than trusting that randomization “looked right.”

Why: QA should expose systematic imbalance before it becomes a research problem.
Decision 04

Keep the participant’s mental model small.

The interaction was designed around a few repeated rules instead of exposing the machinery underneath the experiment.

Why: when the system is complex, the user’s job should become simpler — not equally complex.
Reliability as UX

The interface had to protect the experiment.

A good participant experience was not separate from research quality. Clear instruction, recovery states, timing logs, and counterbalance checks all reduced avoidable sources of noise.

05 / reflection

What this changed about how I think about product design.

Interfaces do not simply display a system; they shape the behavior we observe from people using it. This project made me more deliberate about instruction, error prevention, recovery, and instrumentation — especially in experiences where a “small” interaction detail can change the quality of the evidence downstream.

If I extended this work: I would run a short comprehension test on the onboarding flow, instrument hesitation and error points, and compare instruction variants before freezing the study experience.
